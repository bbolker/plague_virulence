\title{Epidemic Burnout under General Susceptible Recruitment}
\author{Draft manuscript}
\date{September 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Epidemic burnout occurs when a pathogen becomes extinct after causing a major epidemic wave, before it can cause a subsequent wave. Parsons et al. approximated the probability of epidemic burnout by matching a deterministic epidemic trajectory to a stochastic branching process at low prevalence. Existing asymptotic burnout theory for the susceptible--infectious--recovered model with vital dynamics assumes linear susceptible replenishment. Motivated by the possibility of density-dependent recruitment, particularly in ecological host populations, we replace the linear term by a family of susceptible recruitment functions that interpolates continuously between linear and logistic replenishment.
In the limit of slow susceptible recruitment, the leading-order trajectory of the first epidemic wave is unchanged by the recruitment law. The effect of the recruitment function appears instead in the post-wave decline, where differences in replenishment dynamics produce small corrections to the susceptible trajectory that can strongly affect how deeply infection falls before susceptible replenishment permits recovery. We derive first- and second-order asymptotic approximations for this post-epidemic trajectory and for the state at which infection enters the low-prevalence regime. We then use these deterministic approximations as input to Kendall's time-inhomogeneous branching-process calculation to obtain the probability of extinction during the first epidemic trough. Matching the deterministic and stochastic descriptions in their common regime of validity yields a leading-order prediction for the trough depth and burnout probability.
Comparisons with stochastic simulations show that our approximations are accurate over a biologically reasonable range of parameters. We show that the probability of persistence decreases as susceptible replenishment changes from linear to logistic. However, persistence can depend nonmonotonically on the basic reproduction number \(\Rzero\).

 The analysis extends the deterministic--stochastic burnout framework to a broader family of susceptible-recruitment laws and identifies both quantitative and qualitative ways in which recruitment shape modifies survival through the post-wave trough.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large epidemic outbreaks can end in two qualitatively different ways. Infection may persist after the first epidemic wave and recover as susceptible hosts are replenished, or it may disappear while prevalence is still very low. The second outcome is often described as epidemic burnout. Full stochastic simulation can capture both the epidemic wave and the subsequent extinction process, but repeated simulation becomes costly when persistence probabilities must be evaluated across large parameter ranges. A useful approximation is therefore to describe the macroscopic epidemic wave deterministically and treat the low-prevalence phase stochastically.

Parsons and Earn developed uniform asymptotic phase-plane approximations for the SIR model with vital dynamics, and Parsons, Bolker, Dushoff, and Earn subsequently coupled this deterministic structure to a time-inhomogeneous branching-process calculation of first-trough burnout probability \cite{ParsonsEarn2024,ParsonsEtAl2024}. In that setting susceptible replenishment is linear. Here we ask how the same analytical framework changes when susceptible recruitment is density dependent. Standard human-disease SIR models often assume that new susceptible hosts are added at a constant rate and that deaths occur at a constant per-capita rate, giving linear susceptible replenishment. However, when studying epidemics in animal populations, we may need to account for density-dependent host reproduction. Animal populations, such as rodents, often follow a logistic growth pattern. Their growth slows when approaching carrying capacity because food and other resources become limiting. This motivates extending the burnout framework beyond linear susceptible replenishment to include logistic and intermediate recruitment functions.

To connect linear and logistic susceptible replenishment, we consider the recruitment function
\begin{equation}
g_\theta(S)=r(K-S)\left(\frac{S}{K}\right)^\theta,\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq1.
\label{eq:dimensional-recruitment}
\end{equation}
Here, \(S\) is the number of susceptible hosts, \(r\) is a recruitment-rate parameter, and \(K\) is the reference population size. When \(\theta=0\), recruitment is linear, \(g_0(S)=r(K-S)\). When \(\theta=1\), it takes the logistic form \(g_1(S)=rS(1-S/K)\). This formulation allows us to study how changing the susceptible-recruitment law alters the post-wave trough and the probability of epidemic persistence.

A key simplification is that the leading-order trajectory of the first epidemic wave, obtained by neglecting susceptible recruitment, does not depend on \(\theta\). The effect of the recruitment law appears in the corrections to this trajectory as the epidemic declines toward low prevalence. These corrections determine how deeply infection falls after the first wave and therefore affect the subsequent probability of burnout.

Building on the phase-plane and epidemic-burnout approximations developed in earlier work \cite{ParsonsEarn2024,ParsonsEtAl2024}, we derive the corresponding first- and second-order post-wave corrections for the generalized recruitment law, extend the existing matching and trough approximations to this setting, and use the resulting low-prevalence entry states in Kendall's time-inhomogeneous birth--death calculation to evaluate burnout and persistence probabilities.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:model} introduces the generalized SIR model, defines the post-wave burnout problem, and outlines the matching of deterministic and stochastic descriptions. Section~\ref{sec:phaseplane} develops the leading epidemic trajectory and its first-order recruitment correction. Section~\ref{sec:matching} constructs the local approximation near the end of the epidemic wave and determines its constants by matching to the epidemic trajectory. Section~\ref{sec:action} applies Kendall's time-inhomogeneous birth--death formula to the generalized susceptible recovery path and obtains burnout and persistence probabilities in terms of the boundary-layer entry state. Section~\ref{sec:burnout} estimates that state from the epidemic trajectory, then derives closed-form probability approximations and relates them to the infection trough. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} compares the approximations with stochastic simulations and examines the effect of recruitment shape. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses interpretation, limitations, and possible extensions.

\section{Generalized SIR model and epidemic burnout}
\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Model and parameter regime}

We consider an SIR-type model with susceptible population \(S(t)\) and infectious population \(I(t)\):
\begin{align}
\frac{\dd S}{\dd t} &= g_\theta(S)-\beta\frac{SI}{K},\label{eq:dimensional-models}\\
\frac{\dd I}{\dd t} &= \beta\frac{SI}{K}-\delta I.\label{eq:dimensional-modeli}
\end{align}
The recruitment function \(g_\theta\) is defined in \cref{eq:dimensional-recruitment}, \(\beta\) is the transmission coefficient, and \(\delta\) is the infectious removal rate. We assume that \(\beta\) is constant and does not depend on the total host population size. We introduce the dimensionless variables and parameters
\[
x=\frac{S}{K},\qquad y=\frac{I}{K},\qquad
\tau=\delta t,\qquad \rhoeps=\frac{r}{\delta},\qquad
\Rzero=\frac{\beta}{\delta}.
\]
The model then becomes
\begin{align}
\frac{\dd x}{\dd \tau} &= \rhoeps\hth(x)-\Rzero xy,\label{eq:modelx}\\
\frac{\dd y}{\dd \tau} &= (\Rzero x-1)y,\label{eq:modely}
\end{align}
where \(x\) is the susceptible fraction, \(y\) is the infectious fraction, \(\tau\) is time scaled by the infectious removal rate, \(\Rzero>1\) is the basic reproduction number, and \(0<\rhoeps\ll1\) is the susceptible-recruitment rate divided by the infectious removal rate. Recruitment is
\begin{equation}
\hth(x)=(1-x)x^\theta,\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq1.
\label{eq:hth}
\end{equation}
For \(\theta=0\), \(\hth(x)=1-x\) is the standard linear replenishment law. For \(\theta=1\), \(\hth(x)=x(1-x)\) is logistic replenishment. At small susceptible density,\footnote{Throughout, \(f\sim g\) denotes asymptotic equivalence, meaning that \(f/g\to1\) in the stated limit, whereas \(\approx\) denotes an approximation without asserting this limiting relation.}
\[
\hth(x)\sim x^\theta,\qquad x\to0^+,
\]
so \(\theta\) controls the low-density dependence of recruitment.

Note that our recruitment law is different from the classical theta-logistic form \(x(1-x^\vartheta)\), where \(\vartheta>0\) \cite{GilpinCaseAyala1976}. The classical model retains approximately linear growth at low population densities, whereas our family allows different low-density recruitment behaviors. In particular, our family recovers linear replenishment, \(h_0(x)=1-x\), at \(\theta=0\), so replenishment remains nonzero even when no susceptible hosts are present. But the classical theta-logistic family does not include this linear form, and for any \(\vartheta>0\), replenishment is always zero when no susceptible hosts are present. Both expressions reduce to \(x(1-x)\) when their shape parameters equal one.

The infectious growth threshold is
\begin{equation}
\xs=\frac{1}{\Rzero}.
\label{eq:xstar}
\end{equation}
For \(y>0\), the infectious population increases when \(x>\xs\) and decreases when \(x<\xs\), as follows from \cref{eq:modely}.
The endemic equilibrium has susceptible fraction \(\xs\) and infectious fraction
\begin{equation}
y_*=\rhoeps\hth(\xs)=\rhoeps(\Rzero-1)\Rzero^{-(1+\theta)}.
\label{eq:ystar}
\end{equation}

Throughout the main derivation we consider
\begin{equation}
0<\rhoeps\ll1,\qquad \Rzero>1\ \text{fixed},\qquad K\gg1.
\label{eq:regime}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Burnout and deterministic--stochastic matching}
\label{sec:framework}
Following Parsons et al.\ \cite{ParsonsEtAl2024}, we use a deterministic approximation for the epidemic wave and a birth--death approximation in the low-prevalence region, which we call the boundary layer. We combine these descriptions to estimate the probability of burnout conditional on successful epidemic establishment.

Above the low-prevalence region, we approximate the epidemic trajectory deterministically. This requires the infection count to remain large compared with one individual,
\begin{equation}
Ky\gg1,\qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad y\gg K^{-1}.
\label{eq:deterministic-count-condition}
\end{equation}

Within the boundary layer, we describe infection by a time-inhomogeneous birth--death process while retaining a deterministic susceptible recovery path. We neglect susceptible loss through infection when
\begin{equation}
\Rzero xy\ll\rhoeps\hth(x).
\label{eq:low-prevalence-loss-condition}
\end{equation}
For this low-prevalence approximation, we use \(y\ll y_*\).

We choose a boundary-layer threshold \(\yin\) where both descriptions apply. It must therefore satisfy
\begin{equation}
K^{-1}\ll\yin\ll y_*,
\label{eq:matching-overlap}
\end{equation}
where the subscript BL denotes boundary layer.

If the post-peak decline reaches \(y=\yin\), let \(\tau_{\rm BL}\) denote the time of the first downward crossing. We define the corresponding susceptible fraction by
\begin{equation}
\xin:=x(\tau_{\rm BL}).
\label{eq:matching-state}
\end{equation}
The state \((\xin,\yin)\) supplies the susceptible fraction \(\xin\) and the infection count \(K\yin\) for the stochastic extinction calculation.

The calculation proceeds in three steps. First, we determine the epidemic trajectory and its recruitment corrections, then construct a local approximation that remains useful as infection declines toward the boundary-layer threshold. Next, we use this trajectory to estimate \(\xin\) and describe susceptible recovery in the low-prevalence phase. Finally, we calculate the burnout probability using a time-inhomogeneous birth--death process starting from the state \((\xin,\yin)\).

\section{Phase-plane asymptotics under generalized susceptible recruitment}
\label{sec:phaseplane}
We describe the deterministic epidemic trajectory in the phase plane by \(y=y(x;\rhoeps)\), with \(\Rzero\) and \(\theta\) held fixed. For small \(\rhoeps\), we seek an expansion
\begin{equation}
y(x;\rhoeps)=F(x)+\rhoeps Y_1(x)+O(\rhoeps^2).
\label{eq:outerexp}
\end{equation}
Our goal in this section is to determine the leading-order trajectory \(F(x)\) and the first-order correction \(Y_1(x)\).

\subsection{Universal leading epidemic geometry}
We first determine the leading-order trajectory \(F(x)\) by neglecting susceptible recruitment. Setting \(\rhoeps=0\) in \cref{eq:modelx,eq:modely} gives
\[
\frac{\dd y}{\dd x}=\frac{1}{\Rzero x}-1.
\]
Starting from a naive invasion near \((x,y)=(1,0)\), integration gives the leading-order trajectory
\begin{equation}
y=F(x)=1-x+\frac{1}{\Rzero}\log x.
\label{eq:F}
\end{equation}
The epidemic peak occurs at \(x=\xs\). We define \(\xf\) as the susceptible fraction remaining at the end of the epidemic in the absence of recruitment. The final epidemic size, expressed as the fraction of the population infected during the epidemic, is \(1-\xf\). The value \(\xf\) is the root of \(F(\xf)=0\) satisfying \(0<\xf<\xs\), given by
\begin{equation}
\xf=-\frac{1}{\Rzero}W_0(-\Rzero e^{-\Rzero}),
\label{eq:xf}
\end{equation}
where \(W_0\) is the principal branch of the Lambert \(W\) function.

The leading-order trajectory describes the overall shape of the first epidemic wave, but is not sufficiently accurate on its own for calculating the burnout probability. When susceptible recruitment is included, the susceptible fraction at a given infectious fraction differs from that predicted by the leading-order trajectory. The later burnout approximation depends exponentially on this susceptible fraction, with an exponent scaled by \(1/\rhoeps\). An \(O(\rhoeps)\) change in the susceptible fraction can therefore produce an \(O(1)\) change in the exponent and a multiplicative change in the predicted infection trough. We next derive the first-order correction to account for these effects.

\subsection{First-order correction to the epidemic trajectory}
To determine \(Y_1(x)\), we substitute the trajectory expansion into the exact phase-plane equation and equate powers of \(\rhoeps\). Dividing \cref{eq:modely} by \cref{eq:modelx} gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd y}{\dd x}=
\frac{(\Rzero x-1)y}{\rhoeps\hth(x)-\Rzero xy}.
\label{eq:phaseplane}
\end{equation}
We first construct the trajectory expansion in the original variables \(x\) and \(y\). This is called the outer expansion: it describes the epidemic trajectory away from regions where the expansion ceases to be valid and a rescaling of the variables is needed. We will identify and treat these regions below.
To derive the correction equation, we first rewrite \cref{eq:phaseplane} as
\[
\bigl[\rhoeps\hth(x)-\Rzero xy\bigr]y'=(\Rzero x-1)y,
\]
where primes denote differentiation with respect to \(x\). Substituting the trajectory expansion \cref{eq:outerexp} and its derivative gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\Bigl[-\Rzero xF+\rhoeps\bigl(\hth-\Rzero xY_1\bigr)\Bigr]
\Bigl[F'+\rhoeps Y_1'\Bigr]\\
&\qquad=(\Rzero x-1)\bigl[F+\rhoeps Y_1\bigr]+O(\rhoeps^2),
\end{aligned}
\]
with all functions evaluated at \(x\). The coefficients of each power of \(\rhoeps\) must be equal on both sides. At order one,
\[
-\Rzero xFF'=(\Rzero x-1)F,
\]
which recovers the leading-order equation \(F'=1/(\Rzero x)-1\).

At order \(\rhoeps\),
\[
\bigl(\hth-\Rzero xY_1\bigr)F'-\Rzero xF Y_1'
=(\Rzero x-1)Y_1.
\]
Using \(-\Rzero xF'=\Rzero x-1\), the terms containing \(Y_1\) cancel, leaving
\[
Y_1'(x)=\frac{\hth(x)F'(x)}{\Rzero xF(x)}
=-\frac{\hth(x)(\Rzero x-1)}{\Rzero^2x^2F(x)}.
\]
For invasion into a fully susceptible population with vanishing initial infectious fraction, the trajectory approaches \((1,0)\). Since \(F(1)=0\), the correction satisfies \(Y_1(1)=0\). Thus, the first-order correction is determined by
\begin{equation}
Y_1'(x)=-\frac{\hth(x)(\Rzero x-1)}{\Rzero^2x^2F(x)},
\qquad Y_1(1)=0.
\label{eq:Y1prime}
\end{equation}
There is a singularity at \(x=1\) in the right-hand side of \cref{eq:Y1prime}, since \(F(1)=0\). We next show that this singularity is removable. Let \(s=1-x\). Expanding \cref{eq:F,eq:hth} gives
\begin{equation}
F(1-s)=\left(1-\frac1{\Rzero}\right)s+O(s^2),
\qquad \hth(1-s)=s+O(s^2).
\label{eq:initial-endpoint-expansions}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:initial-endpoint-expansions} into \cref{eq:Y1prime} yields
\begin{equation}
Y_1'(1-s)=-\frac1{\Rzero}+O(s).
\label{eq:Y1prime-initial-limit}
\end{equation}
Using the initial condition \(Y_1(1)=0\) in \cref{eq:Y1prime} and integrating \cref{eq:Y1prime-initial-limit}, we obtain
\[
Y_1(1-s)=\frac{s}{\Rzero}+O(s^2).
\]
The singularity is therefore removable, and extending the right-hand side by continuity gives
\begin{equation}
Y_1'(1)=-\frac1{\Rzero}.
\label{eq:first-order-endpoint-value}
\end{equation}

\section{Singular matching near the post-epidemic state}
\label{sec:matching}
We now examine the correction terms as the infectious fraction \(y\) decreases and the susceptible fraction \(x\) approaches \(\xf\). Unlike the removable singularities at \(x=1\), the corrections become singular near \(\xf\), so the outer expansion cannot describe the transition to low prevalence on its own. We construct a local approximation near \(\xf\) and match it to the outer expansion. The resulting approximation allows us to estimate the susceptible fraction when infection enters the low-prevalence region.

\subsection{First-order singularity and the matching constant \texorpdfstring{\(\Cth\)}{C theta}}
We first examine the behavior of the first-order correction \(Y_1(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(\xf\) from above. This behavior will be used to match the outer expansion to the local approximation near \(\xf\).
To simplify the notation, we define
\begin{equation}
G_\theta(x)=\Rzero Y_1(x).
\label{eq:Gtheta-definition}
\end{equation}
Multiplying \cref{eq:Y1prime} by \(\Rzero\) gives
\begin{equation}
G_\theta'(x)=\frac{(\xs-x)\hth(x)}{x^2F(x)}.
\label{eq:Gtheta}
\end{equation}
Since \(F(\xf)=0\) and \cref{eq:F} gives
\[
F'(\xf)=\frac{\xs-\xf}{\xf}>0,
\]
expanding \(F(x)\) about \(\xf\) yields
\begin{equation}
F(x)=F'(\xf)(x-\xf)+O((x-\xf)^2).
\label{eq:F-near-xf}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:F-near-xf} into \cref{eq:Gtheta} then gives
\begin{equation}
G_\theta'(x)=\frac{\hth(\xf)}{\xf}\frac1{x-\xf}+O(1),
\qquad x\to\xf^+.
\label{eq:Gtheta-singular-term}
\end{equation}
We define the coefficient of this singular term as
\begin{equation}
\widetilde a_\theta=\frac{\hth(\xf)}{\xf}.
\label{eq:atilde}
\end{equation}
Integrating the \(1/(x-\xf)\) term in \cref{eq:Gtheta-singular-term} shows that \(G_\theta(x)\), and hence \(Y_1(x)\), has a logarithmic singularity at \(\xf\).
To determine the constant accompanying the logarithmic term in \(G_\theta(x)\), we subtract the singular term in \cref{eq:Gtheta-singular-term} from \(G_\theta'(x)\). The resulting integrand has a finite limit at \(\xf\). We therefore define
\begin{equation}
J_\theta=
\int_{\xf}^{1}
\left[
\frac{(\xs-u)\hth(u)}{u^2F(u)}-
\frac{\widetilde a_\theta}{u-\xf}
\right]\dd u.
\label{eq:Jtheta}
\end{equation}
Using \(G_\theta(1)=\Rzero Y_1(1)=0\), we integrate \cref{eq:Gtheta} to obtain
\[
G_\theta(x)=-\int_x^1G_\theta'(u)\,\dd u.
\]
Therefore, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
G_\theta(x)
={}&-\int_x^1\left[G_\theta'(u)-\frac{\widetilde a_\theta}{u-\xf}
+\frac{\widetilde a_\theta}{u-\xf}\right]\dd u\\
={}&-\int_x^1\left[G_\theta'(u)-\frac{\widetilde a_\theta}{u-\xf}\right]\dd u
-\widetilde a_\theta\int_x^1\frac{\dd u}{u-\xf}\\
={}&-\int_x^1\left[G_\theta'(u)-\frac{\widetilde a_\theta}{u-\xf}\right]\dd u
+\widetilde a_\theta\log\frac{x-\xf}{1-\xf}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Taking \(x\to\xf^+\) and using \cref{eq:Jtheta}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
G_\theta(x)=\widetilde a_\theta\log\frac{x-\xf}{1-\xf}-J_\theta+o(1),
\qquad x\to\xf^+.
\label{eq:Gtheta-finite-part}
\end{equation}

To estimate the susceptible fraction at a specified low infectious fraction, we now express \(x\) as a function of \(y\). We use the part of the trajectory after the epidemic peak, where \(y\) decreases as \(x\) decreases toward \(\xf\). Writing \(x=X(y;\rhoeps)\), we seek the inverse expansion
\begin{equation}
X(y;\rhoeps)=X_0(y)+\rhoeps X_1(y)+O(\rhoeps^2),
\qquad F(X_0(y))=y.
\label{eq:inverseexp}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:inverseexp} into \cref{eq:outerexp} and retaining terms through first order in \(\rhoeps\) gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
y
&=F(X_0+\rhoeps X_1+O(\rhoeps^2))
+\rhoeps Y_1(X_0+\rhoeps X_1+O(\rhoeps^2))+O(\rhoeps^2)\\
&=F(X_0)+\rhoeps\bigl[F'(X_0)X_1+Y_1(X_0)\bigr]+O(\rhoeps^2).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since \(F(X_0(y))=y\), the coefficient of \(\rhoeps\) must vanish:
\[
F'(X_0)X_1+Y_1(X_0)=0.
\]
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
X_1(y)=-\frac{Y_1(X_0(y))}{F'(X_0(y))}.
\label{eq:X1}
\end{equation}
To evaluate \cref{eq:X1} as \(y\to0^+\), we first expand \(F(X_0(y))=y\) about \(\xf\). Using \(F(\xf)=0\), we obtain
\[
y=F'(\xf)\bigl(X_0(y)-\xf\bigr)+O\bigl((X_0(y)-\xf)^2\bigr).
\]
Since \(F'(\xf)>0\), solving for \(X_0(y)-\xf\) gives
\begin{equation}
X_0(y)-\xf=\frac{y}{F'(\xf)}+O(y^2).
\label{eq:X0-near-xf}
\end{equation}
Setting \(x=X_0(y)\) in \cref{eq:Gtheta-finite-part} and using \cref{eq:X0-near-xf} gives
\begin{equation}
G_\theta(X_0(y))=\widetilde a_\theta\log\frac{y}{(1-\xf)F'(\xf)}-J_\theta+o(1).
\label{eq:Gtheta-X0}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:Gtheta-X0} and \(Y_1=G_\theta/\Rzero\) from \cref{eq:Gtheta-definition} into \cref{eq:X1}, with \(F'(X_0(y))=F'(\xf)+O(y)\), yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
X_1(y)
&=-\frac{1}{\Rzero F'(\xf)}\left[\widetilde a_\theta\log\frac{y}{(1-\xf)F'(\xf)}-J_\theta\right]+o(1)\\
&=\frac{\widetilde a_\theta}{\Rzero F'(\xf)}
\log\frac{(1-\xf)F'(\xf)\exp(J_\theta/\widetilde a_\theta)}{y}+o(1).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:X1-before-constants}
\end{equation}
We define the coefficient and the constant inside the logarithm in \cref{eq:X1-before-constants} as
\begin{equation}
a=\frac{\widetilde a_\theta}{\Rzero F'(\xf)}
=\frac{\hth(\xf)}{1-\Rzero\xf},
\label{eq:a}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\Cth=(1-\xf)F'(\xf)\exp\left(\frac{J_\theta}{\widetilde a_\theta}\right)
=(1-\xf)\frac{\xs-\xf}{\xf}\exp\left(\frac{J_\theta}{\widetilde a_\theta}\right).
\label{eq:Ctheta}
\end{equation}
Then \cref{eq:X1-before-constants} becomes
\begin{equation}
X_1(y)=a\log\frac{\Cth}{y}+o(1),\qquad y\to0^+.
\label{eq:X1asymp}
\end{equation}
The matching constant \(\Cth\) depends only on \(\Rzero\) and \(\theta\), and is independent of \(\rhoeps\) and \(K\). For \(\theta=0\), \cref{eq:Ctheta} recovers the refined first-order matching constant of Parsons et al. \cite{ParsonsEtAl2024}.

\subsection{Breakdown of the outer inverse expansion and corner scaling}
We now compare the first-order correction \(\rhoeps X_1(y)\) with the leading displacement \(X_0(y)-\xf\). This comparison identifies where the outer expansion loses its ordering and a local approximation is needed.
Near \(y=0\),
\[
X_0(y)-\xf\sim\frac{y}{F'(\xf)},
\qquad
\rhoeps X_1(y)\sim\rhoeps a\log\frac{\Cth}{y}.
\]
Using \cref{eq:X0-near-xf,eq:X1asymp}, the ratio of the first-order correction to the leading displacement satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{\rhoeps|X_1(y)|}{|X_0(y)-\xf|}
\sim\frac{\rhoeps aF'(\xf)}{y}\left|\log\frac{\Cth}{y}\right|,
\qquad y\to0^+.
\label{eq:outer-correction-ratio}
\end{equation}
For the first-order term to remain a correction to the leading displacement, this ratio must be small. As \(y\) decreases, the ratio grows, so the outer expansion no longer provides an ordered approximation near \(\xf\). We therefore construct a local approximation in this region.
Rewriting \cref{eq:phaseplane} with \(y\) as the independent variable gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd x}{\dd y}=\frac{\rhoeps\hth(x)-\Rzero xy}{(\Rzero x-1)y}.
\label{eq:inverse-phaseplane}
\end{equation}
Near \(\xf\), the recruitment term \(\rhoeps\hth(x)\) and the infection-induced depletion term \(\Rzero xy\) are of comparable size when \(y=O(\rhoeps)\). To retain both terms in the local approximation, we set
\begin{equation}
y=\rhoeps v,
\qquad
x=\xf+\rhoeps u_1(v)+O(\rhoeps^2).
\label{eq:corner}
\end{equation}
These variables describe the corner layer near the turn in the susceptible trajectory, following Parsons and Earn \cite{ParsonsEarn2024}.
We now substitute \cref{eq:corner} into \cref{eq:inverse-phaseplane} to determine \(u_1(v)\). With \(\rhoeps\) held fixed, differentiation with respect to \(v\) gives
\[
\frac{\dd y}{\dd v}=\rhoeps,
\qquad
\frac{\dd x}{\dd v}=\rhoeps u_1'(v)+O(\rhoeps^2).
\]
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd x}{\dd y}=\frac{\dd x/\dd v}{\dd y/\dd v}
=u_1'(v)+O(\rhoeps).
\label{eq:corner-derivative}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:corner} into the numerator and denominator of \cref{eq:inverse-phaseplane} gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\rhoeps\hth(x)-\Rzero xy
&=\rhoeps\bigl[\hth(\xf)-\Rzero\xf v\bigr]
+\rhoeps^2\bigl[\hth'(\xf)-\Rzero v\bigr]u_1(v)+\cdots,\\
(\Rzero x-1)y
&=\rhoeps(\Rzero\xf-1)v+\rhoeps^2\Rzero u_1(v)v+\cdots.
\end{aligned}
\]
Dividing these expressions and comparing the leading terms with \cref{eq:corner-derivative}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
u_1'(v)=\frac{\hth(\xf)-\Rzero\xf v}{(\Rzero\xf-1)v}
=\frac{\Rzero\xf}{1-\Rzero\xf}
-\frac{\hth(\xf)}{1-\Rzero\xf}\frac1v.
\label{eq:u1prime-full}
\end{equation}
The coefficient of \(1/v\) is \(a\), defined in \cref{eq:a}. We define the remaining coefficient as
\begin{equation}
b=\frac{\Rzero\xf}{1-\Rzero\xf}.
\label{eq:b}
\end{equation}
Then \cref{eq:u1prime-full} becomes
\begin{equation}
u_1'(v)=b-\frac{a}{v}.
\label{eq:u1prime}
\end{equation}
Integrating \cref{eq:u1prime} with respect to \(v\) gives
\begin{equation}
u_1(v)=bv-a\log v+B_1(\rhoeps).
\label{eq:u1-integrated}
\end{equation}
Here \(B_1(\rhoeps)\) is an integration constant that may depend on \(\rhoeps\), which is held fixed during integration. We determine this constant by matching the corner approximation to the outer expansion in a region where both are valid.

Using \cref{eq:inverseexp,eq:X0-near-xf,eq:X1asymp}, the outer expansion gives
\begin{equation}
x-\xf=\frac{y}{F'(\xf)}+\rhoeps a\log\frac{\Cth}{y}+\cdots.
\label{eq:u1-outer-matching}
\end{equation}
Since
\[
\frac{1}{F'(\xf)}=\frac{\Rzero\xf}{1-\Rzero\xf}=b,
\]
substituting \(y=\rhoeps v\) into \cref{eq:u1-outer-matching} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
x-\xf
&=\rhoeps bv+\rhoeps a\log\frac{\Cth}{\rhoeps v}+\cdots\\
&=\rhoeps\left[bv-a\log v+a\log\frac{\Cth}{\rhoeps}\right]+\cdots.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:u1-outer-local}
\end{equation}
Meanwhile, substituting \cref{eq:u1-integrated} into \cref{eq:corner} gives
\begin{equation}
x-\xf=\rhoeps\left[bv-a\log v+B_1(\rhoeps)\right]+\cdots.
\label{eq:u1-corner-matching}
\end{equation}
Comparing \cref{eq:u1-outer-local,eq:u1-corner-matching} determines
\begin{equation}
B_1(\rhoeps)=a\log\frac{\Cth}{\rhoeps}.
\label{eq:B1}
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\[
\begin{aligned}
u_1(v)&=bv-a\log v+a\log\frac{\Cth}{\rhoeps}\\
&=bv+a\log\frac{\Cth}{\rhoeps v}.
\end{aligned}
\]
We define
\begin{equation}
L=\log\frac{\Cth}{y}=\log\frac{\Cth}{\rhoeps v},
\label{eq:corner-L}
\end{equation}
so the matched first-order corner approximation can be written as
\begin{equation}
u_1(v)=aL+bv.
\label{eq:u1}
\end{equation}
The first-order trajectory is therefore
\begin{equation}
x\approx\xf+\rhoeps aL+by.
\label{eq:first-order-corner-trajectory}
\end{equation}

\section{Burnout probability from the boundary-layer entry state}
\label{sec:action}
We now estimate the burnout probability starting from the state \((\xin,\yin)\) at entry into the boundary layer.

\subsection{The birth--death process and Kendall's formula}
We take \(\tau=0\) at entry into the boundary layer, with susceptible fraction \(\xin\) and \(m=K\yin\) infectious individuals. In the boundary layer, we neglect susceptible loss through infection, \(\Rzero xy\), relative to recruitment, \(\rhoeps\hth(x)\), as specified in \cref{eq:low-prevalence-loss-condition}. We therefore approximate susceptible recovery by the solution of
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd x}{\dd\tau}=\rhoeps\hth(x),\qquad x(0)=\xin.
\label{eq:branching-background}
\end{equation}
Under this approximation, when there are \(n\) infectious individuals, new infections occur at total rate \(n\Rzero x(\tau)\), and removals occur at total rate \(n\). Both rates are proportional to \(n\), and the infection rate varies with time through the deterministic susceptible fraction \(x(\tau)\). The infectious population therefore follows a time-inhomogeneous linear birth--death process.
For a time-inhomogeneous linear birth--death process with per-capita birth and removal rates \(b(\tau)\) and \(d(\tau)\), Kendall's formula \cite{Kendall1948} gives the eventual extinction probability starting from one individual as
\begin{equation}
q_1=\frac{\mathcal{I}}{1+\mathcal{I}},
\label{eq:q1}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}(\xin)=\int_0^\infty d(\tau)
\exp\!\left[-\int_0^\tau\bigl(b(s)-d(s)\bigr)\dd s\right]\dd\tau.
\label{eq:Kendall-time}
\end{equation}
For our epidemic approximation, these rates are
\begin{equation}
b(\tau)=\Rzero x(\tau),\qquad d(\tau)=1,
\label{eq:branching-rates}
\end{equation}
where \(x(\tau)\) is given by \cref{eq:branching-background}.

\subsection{Evaluating the Kendall integral}
We now evaluate \cref{eq:Kendall-time} by changing the integration variable from time to the susceptible fraction, using \cref{eq:branching-background}.

Using the susceptible recovery equation \cref{eq:branching-background} and the infectious-fraction equation \cref{eq:modely}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd\log y}{\dd x}
=\frac{\dd\log y/\dd\tau}{\dd x/\dd\tau}
\approx\frac{\Rzero x-1}{\rhoeps\hth(x)}.
\label{eq:logy-versus-x}
\end{equation}
We define the action function \(\Ath(x)\) by
\begin{equation}
\Ath'(x)=\frac{1-\Rzero x}{\hth(x)}
=\frac{1-\Rzero x}{(1-x)x^\theta}.
\label{eq:actionprime}
\end{equation}
The relation above then becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd\log y}{\dd x}\approx-\frac{1}{\rhoeps}\Ath'(x).
\label{eq:logyaction}
\end{equation}
Integrating \cref{eq:logyaction} between two susceptible fractions \(z\) and \(x\) along the low-prevalence trajectory gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{y(x)}{y(z)}
&\approx-\frac1{\rhoeps}\int_z^x\Ath'(u)\dd u\\
&=-\frac{\Ath(x)-\Ath(z)}{\rhoeps}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:logy-action-difference}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
y(x)\approx y(z)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\Ath(x)-\Ath(z)}{\rhoeps}\right].
\label{eq:y-action-propagation}
\end{equation}
Thus, the action difference determines the exponential change in infectious prevalence. From \cref{eq:actionprime}, this difference is
\begin{equation}
\Ath(x)-\Ath(z)=\int_z^x\frac{1-\Rzero u}{(1-u)u^\theta}\dd u.
\label{eq:actiondifference}
\end{equation}

For the susceptible background in \cref{eq:branching-background}, changing variables to \(X=x(\tau)\) gives
\begin{equation}
\dd\tau=\frac{\dd X}{\rhoeps\hth(X)},\qquad
x(0)=\xin,\qquad \lim_{\tau\to\infty}x(\tau)=1.
\label{eq:Kendall-time-change}
\end{equation}
Using \cref{eq:branching-rates,eq:Kendall-time-change,eq:actionprime}, the accumulated growth rate becomes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\tau\bigl(b(s)-d(s)\bigr)\dd s
&=\int_{\xin}^{x(\tau)}\frac{\Rzero X-1}{\rhoeps\hth(X)}\dd X\\
&=-\frac{\Ath(x(\tau))-\Ath(\xin)}{\rhoeps}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:Kendall-growth-integral}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:Kendall-time-change,eq:Kendall-growth-integral} and \(d(\tau)=1\) from \cref{eq:branching-rates} into \cref{eq:Kendall-time} gives
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}(\xin)=
\int_{\xin}^{1}
\frac{1}{\rhoeps\hth(X)}
\exp\left[\frac{\Ath(X)-\Ath(\xin)}{\rhoeps}\right]\dd X.
\label{eq:Kendall}
\end{equation}

For \(\theta=0,\tfrac12,1\), integrating \cref{eq:actionprime} gives
\begin{align}
A_0(x)&=\Rzero x+(\Rzero-1)\log(1-x),\\
A_{1/2}(x)&=2\Rzero\sqrt{x}-2(\Rzero-1)\operatorname{artanh}\sqrt{x},\\
A_1(x)&=\log x+(\Rzero-1)\log(1-x),
\end{align}
up to additive constants.

\subsection{Burnout and persistence probabilities}
In the birth--death approximation, the susceptible fraction \(x(\tau)\) is prescribed deterministically, and infectious individuals produce new infections and are removed independently at the per-capita rates in \cref{eq:branching-rates}. The \(m=K\yin\) infectious individuals present at boundary-layer entry therefore generate independent lineages. Burnout occurs if all these lineages become extinct, giving
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm burn\mid est}\approx q_1^{\,m}
=\left[\frac{\mathcal{I}(\xin)}{1+\mathcal{I}(\xin)}\right]^{K\yin}.
\label{eq:Pburn}
\end{equation}
The conditional persistence probability is therefore
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm persist\mid est}=1-P_{\rm burn\mid est}.
\label{eq:Ppersistcond}
\end{equation}
We define
\begin{equation}
B:=K\yin\log\!\left(1+\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}(\xin)}\right).
\label{eq:B}
\end{equation}
Then
\[
-\log P_{\rm burn\mid est}\approx B,
\qquad
P_{\rm persist\mid est}\approx1-e^{-B}.
\]

The post-wave calculation is conditional on initial epidemic establishment. When \(\Rzero>1\), the early branching approximation gives the establishment probability for a single infectious host introduced into an otherwise susceptible population as \cite{Whittle1955}
\begin{equation}
p_{\rm est}\approx1-\frac{1}{\Rzero},
\label{eq:pest}
\end{equation}
The unconditional persistence probability is therefore
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm persist,uncond}
=p_{\rm est}P_{\rm persist\mid est}
\approx\left(1-\frac{1}{\Rzero}\right)P_{\rm persist\mid est}.
\label{eq:uncond}
\end{equation}


\section{Entry-state estimates and asymptotic burnout probabilities}
\label{sec:burnout}
\subsection{Finite-height entry equations}
We now estimate \(\xin\) at the boundary-layer threshold \(y=\yin\) defined in Section~\ref{sec:framework}. We choose
\begin{equation}
\yin=\sqrt{\frac{y_*}{K}}.
\label{eq:ybl}
\end{equation}
When \(Ky_*\gg1\), this choice satisfies \cref{eq:matching-overlap}.

Evaluating \(L\), defined in \cref{eq:corner-L}, at \(y=\yin\), we denote its value by
\begin{equation}
L_{\rm BL}:=\left.L\right|_{y=\yin}=\log\frac{\Cth}{\yin}.
\label{eq:LBLQ}
\end{equation}
The first-order corner estimate is
\begin{equation}
x_{\rm in}^{(1)}=\xf+\rhoeps aL_{\rm BL}+b\yin.
\label{eq:xindir1}
\end{equation}
We can retain one further order to improve the entry-state estimate; the derivation and resulting approximation are given in Appendix~\ref{app:second-order}.

\subsection{Laplace reduction}
The burnout probability obtained above still requires numerical evaluation of the Kendall integral \cref{eq:Kendall}. We next use Laplace's method to approximate this integral further.
Laplace's method \cite[Section~6.4]{BenderOrszag1978} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\int_a^b f(X)e^{\phi(X)/\rhoeps}\dd X
={}&f(x_m)e^{\phi(x_m)/\rhoeps}
\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\rhoeps}{\lambda_m}}\\
&\times\left[1+\rhoeps c_{\mathrm{Lap}}+O(\rhoeps^2)\right],
\qquad \rhoeps\to0^+,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:Laplace-standard}
\end{equation}
where \(x_m\) is the unique interior maximum of \(\phi\), \(\lambda_m=-\phi''(x_m)>0\), and
\begin{equation}
c_{\mathrm{Lap}}=
\frac{f''}{2f\lambda_m}
+\frac{f'\phi'''}{2f\lambda_m^2}
+\frac{\phi''''}{8\lambda_m^2}
+\frac{5(\phi''')^2}{24\lambda_m^3}.
\label{eq:Laplace-standard-correction}
\end{equation}
All functions and derivatives in \cref{eq:Laplace-standard-correction} are evaluated at \(x_m\). The expansion requires sufficient smoothness and \(f(x_m)\ne0\). The distances from the maximum to both integration endpoints must be much larger than the width of the peak:
\begin{equation}
x_m-a\gg\sqrt{\frac{\rhoeps}{\lambda_m}},\qquad
b-x_m\gg\sqrt{\frac{\rhoeps}{\lambda_m}}.
\label{eq:Laplace-endpoint-general}
\end{equation}
For the Kendall integral \cref{eq:Kendall}, we take
\begin{equation}
f(X)=\frac1{\hth(X)},\qquad
\phi(X)=\Ath(X)-\Ath(\xin).
\label{eq:Laplace-substitution}
\end{equation}
From \cref{eq:actionprime},
\begin{equation}
\phi'(X)=\frac{1-\Rzero X}{\hth(X)}.
\label{eq:Laplace-phase-derivative}
\end{equation}
Since \(\hth(X)>0\) for \(0<X<1\), \(\phi\) increases for \(X<\xs\) and decreases for \(X>\xs\). Thus, for \(\xin<\xs=1/\Rzero\), its unique maximum lies inside the integration interval at \(x_m=\xs\), with
\begin{equation}
\lambda_m=-\phi''(\xs)=\frac{\Rzero}{\hth(\xs)}.
\label{eq:Laplace-phase-curvature}
\end{equation}
We define
\begin{equation}
\Lambda(\xin):=\frac{\Ath(\xs)-\Ath(\xin)}{\rhoeps},\qquad h_*:=\hth(\xs).
\label{eq:Lambda}
\end{equation}
For our integral, \cref{eq:Laplace-endpoint-general} becomes
\begin{equation}
\xs-\xin\gg\sqrt{\frac{\rhoeps h_*}{\Rzero}},\qquad
1-\xs\gg\sqrt{\frac{\rhoeps h_*}{\Rzero}}.
\label{eq:Laplace-endpoint-conditions}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:Laplace-substitution,eq:Laplace-phase-curvature} into the leading term of \cref{eq:Laplace-standard} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{I}(\xin)&\sim
\frac{e^{\Lambda(\xin)}}{\rhoeps h_*}
\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\rhoeps h_*}{\Rzero}}\\
&=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\Rzero y_*}}\,e^{\Lambda(\xin)},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:LaplaceI}
\end{equation}
where \(y_*=\rhoeps h_*\).
Substituting the derivatives of \(f\) and \(\phi\) into \cref{eq:Laplace-standard-correction} gives
\begin{equation}
c_L=\frac{h_*}{\Rzero}
\left[
\frac{1}{12}\left(\frac{h_*'}{h_*}\right)^2
-\frac18\frac{h_*''}{h_*}
\right],\qquad
h_*'=\hth'(\xs),\quad h_*''=\hth''(\xs).
\label{eq:cL}
\end{equation}
The corrected approximation is therefore
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}(\xin)=
\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\Rzero y_*}}\,e^{\Lambda(\xin)}
\left[1+\rhoeps c_L+O(\rhoeps^2)\right].
\label{eq:LaplaceNext}
\end{equation}
For fixed \(\xin<\xs\), the action difference \(\Ath(\xs)-\Ath(\xin)\) is positive. Hence, \cref{eq:LaplaceI} shows that \(\mathcal{I}(\xin)\) grows exponentially as \(\rhoeps\to0^+\). Therefore, for sufficiently small \(\rhoeps\), \(\mathcal{I}(\xin)\gg1\), and
\begin{equation}
\log\!\left(1+\frac1{\mathcal{I}(\xin)}\right)\sim\frac1{\mathcal{I}(\xin)}.
\label{eq:large-I-log}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:LaplaceI} into \cref{eq:B}, using \cref{eq:large-I-log}, therefore gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
B&\sim\frac{K\yin}{\mathcal{I}(\xin)}\\
&\sim K\yin\sqrt{\frac{\Rzero y_*}{2\pi}}\,e^{-\Lambda(\xin)}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:LaplaceB}
\end{equation}
If \cref{eq:Laplace-endpoint-conditions} is not satisfied, the Kendall integral \cref{eq:Kendall} can instead be evaluated numerically.

\subsection{Boundary-layer-independent trough and burnout exponent}
The first-order estimate \cref{eq:xindir1} is
\begin{equation}
x_{\rm in}^{(1)}
=\xf+\rhoeps a\log\frac{\Cth}{\yin}+b\yin.
\label{eq:BI-entry-repeat}
\end{equation}
For our choice \(\yin=\sqrt{y_*/K}\),
\begin{equation}
\frac{\yin}{\rhoeps}
=\frac{\hth(\xs)}{\sqrt{Ky_*}}\ll1
\qquad\text{when }Ky_*\gg1.
\label{eq:BI-height-scale}
\end{equation}
Taylor expansion about \(\xf\), using \(\Ath'(\xf)=1/a\), gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\Ath(x_{\rm in}^{(1)})-\Ath(\xf)}{\rhoeps}
&=\frac{x_{\rm in}^{(1)}-\xf}{a\rhoeps}
+O\!\left(\frac{(x_{\rm in}^{(1)}-\xf)^2}{\rhoeps}\right)\\
&=\log\frac{\Cth}{\yin}+\frac ba\frac{\yin}{\rhoeps}
+O\!\left(\rhoeps L_{\rm BL}^2+\frac{\yin^2}{\rhoeps}\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:BI-entry-Taylor}
\end{equation}
Since \(\yin/\rhoeps\ll1\), we can neglect the second term in the last line of \cref{eq:BI-entry-Taylor}. To neglect the Taylor remainder as well, we require
\begin{equation}
\rhoeps L_{\rm BL}^2\ll1.
\label{eq:BI-log-condition}
\end{equation}
Under these conditions,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Ath(x_{\rm in}^{(1)})-\Ath(\xf)}{\rhoeps}
=\log\frac{\Cth}{\yin}+o(1).
\label{eq:BIaction}
\end{equation}

In particular, combining this approximation with the leading-order Laplace result \cref{eq:LaplaceB} gives a burnout estimate that is independent of the particular choice of \(\yin\) at leading order. To show this, we write
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Ath(\xs)-\Ath(x_{\rm in}^{(1)})
={}&\dAf\\
&-\left[\Ath(x_{\rm in}^{(1)})-\Ath(\xf)\right],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:BI-action-split}
\end{equation}
where we define
\begin{equation}
\dAf:=\Ath(\xs)-\Ath(\xf).
\label{eq:action-barrier}
\end{equation}
Using \cref{eq:BIaction}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{\Ath(\xs)-\Ath(x_{\rm in}^{(1)})}{\rhoeps}\right]
&=e^{-\dAf/\rhoeps}\frac{\Cth}{\yin}\,e^{o(1)}\\
&\sim\frac{\Cth}{\yin}e^{-\dAf/\rhoeps}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:BI-exponential-cancellation}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:BI-exponential-cancellation} into \cref{eq:LaplaceB} cancels \(\yin\), giving
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
B&\sim K\yin\sqrt{\frac{\Rzero y_*}{2\pi}}\,
\frac{\Cth}{\yin}e^{-\dAf/\rhoeps}\\
&=K\Cth\sqrt{\frac{\Rzero y_*}{2\pi}}\,e^{-\dAf/\rhoeps}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:BI-B-cancellation}
\end{equation}
We denote this threshold-independent approximation by
\begin{equation}
B_{\BI}:=K\Cth\sqrt{\frac{\Rzero y_*}{2\pi}}\,e^{-\dAf/\rhoeps},
\label{eq:BBI}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm persist\mid est}^{\BI}:=1-e^{-B_{\BI}}.
\label{eq:PBI}
\end{equation}

The same approximation also gives an estimate of the minimum infectious fraction along the deterministic trajectory. Setting \(x=\xs\) and \(z=\xin\) in \cref{eq:y-action-propagation} gives
\begin{equation}
y_{\min}\approx\yin
\exp\!\left[-\frac{\Ath(\xs)-\Ath(\xin)}{\rhoeps}\right].
\label{eq:yminentry}
\end{equation}
Using \cref{eq:BI-exponential-cancellation}, this simplifies to
\begin{equation}
y_{\min}\approx\Cth e^{-\dAf/\rhoeps}.
\label{eq:yminBI}
\end{equation}

\section{Numerical validation and the effect of recruitment shape}
\label{sec:numerics}
We use the BI approximation for the results below because it gives the best overall agreement with simulations on the comparison grid and covers a broader parameter range than the first- and second-order entry approximations (Appendix~\ref{app:approximation-comparison}).

We first compare the analytical predictions with stochastic simulations, then examine the sources of approximation error. Next, we investigate the effect of the recruitment law on persistence. To validate the predicted conditional persistence probabilities, we compared the boundary-layer-independent prediction \cref{eq:PBI} with adaptive tau-leap simulations of the full stochastic epidemic.

\subsection{Stochastic validation across transmission and population size}
Figure~\ref{fig:main-persistence} shows the conditional persistence probability as a function of \(\Rzero\) at \(\rhoeps=0.01\), for \(10^6\leq K\leq10^9\). The boundary-layer-independent approximation agrees closely with the stochastic simulation results over much of the tested range, but substantial discrepancies occur at high \(\Rzero\) for some combinations of \(\theta\) and \(K\).

\subsection{Patterns of conditional persistence}
Figure~\ref{fig:main-persistence} shows that increasing \(\theta\) decreases the conditional persistence probability when other parameters are fixed. Biologically, a larger \(\theta\) reduces susceptible replenishment after the epidemic, slowing susceptible recovery and making extinction more likely during the first trough. Within the range shown in Figure~\ref{fig:main-persistence}, persistence is nonmonotonic in \(\Rzero\), first increasing, then decreasing, and subsequently recovering (the initial increase is more visible at \(K=10^6\); for larger \(K\), persistence is already close to one near \(\Rzero=1\), making this increase less noticeable). For \(\theta=0\), persistence recovers relatively early and approaches one as \(\Rzero\) increases. As \(\theta\) increases, this recovery occurs later and proceeds more slowly, or no recovery occurs within the plotted range. As \(\theta\) approaches one, persistence at high \(\Rzero\) is suppressed to nearly zero. This suggests that more transmissible pathogens are more sensitive to susceptible replenishment.

At higher \(\Rzero\), the simulated persistence probability declines again for some parameter combinations because the number of susceptibles may hit zero in some trajectories, whereas the BI prediction remains close to one. The source of this discrepancy is examined in Section~\ref{sec:approximation-errors}. However, biologically reasonable values of \(\Rzero\) do not reach the large-\(\Rzero\) regime where our approximation breaks down.

To examine conditional persistence across different recruitment rates, we plot its dependence on \(\Rzero\) and \(\rhoeps\) in Figure~\ref{fig:conditional-landscape}. Here, we focus only on biologically reasonable values of \(\Rzero\), showing \(\Rzero\le32\), where our approximation remains accurate. The figure shows that the effect of recruitment shape persists over a broader range of recruitment rates. In addition, Figure~\ref{fig:conditional-landscape} shows that increasing \(\rhoeps\) raises the conditional persistence probability. Biologically, faster susceptible replenishment allows infection to resume growth sooner, reducing the risk of extinction during the trough.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
We extend the framework of Parsons et al. for estimating epidemic burnout from linear susceptible replenishment to the recruitment family \(\hth(x)=(1-x)x^\theta\) to account for density-dependent recruitment in animal host populations. The leading-order trajectory of the first epidemic wave is independent of the recruitment law. Recruitment affects the post-wave trajectory through the first- and second-order corrections derived here. We use the corrected trajectory to estimate the entry state for a time-inhomogeneous birth--death process, which gives the extinction probability during the first trough.

In the parameter ranges examined, increasing \(\theta\) reduces conditional persistence. For \(0<x<1\), a larger \(\theta\) gives a lower susceptible recruitment rate. Slower replenishment delays the recovery of susceptibles, leaving infection at low prevalence for longer and increasing the opportunity for extinction. When the persistence probabilities are all close to zero or all close to one, differences between recruitment laws are small, while these differences are more pronounced in the intermediate transition region.

% BEGIN deferred discussion of BI global extrema
% The global effect is richer than this pointwise ordering. Conditional post-wave persistence may already be nonmonotone in \(\Rzero\), whereas the establishment factor \(1-1/\Rzero\) is strictly increasing. The analytical condition \cref{eq:extremumG} shows that extrema of their product occur only when the post-wave contribution decreases rapidly enough to outweigh increasing establishment. At fixed finite \(K\), the unconditional BI probability returns to zero at large \(\Rzero\): algebraically for \(\theta<1\), because \(\Cth\propto\Rzero^{-1}\), and exponentially for \(\theta=1\), because the action grows linearly. Thus \(\theta=1\) is a qualitative boundary in decay rate, not in the limiting probability. At \(\rhoeps=0.01\), increasing \(\theta\) moves the finite-region maximum and minimum in opposite directions; a minimum leaving a plotting window is not evidence of annihilation. At larger \(\rhoeps\), \(G=G_{\Rzero}=0\) confirms creation of an additional pair through a genuine saddle-node. The shape map shows that this pair is not automatic for \(\theta<1\), and that its creation value \(\theta_c\) depends on \((\rhoeps,K)\) rather than marking a generic onset of nonmonotonicity.
%
% END deferred discussion of BI global extrema

Our results suggest that host populations with similar transmission and recruitment rates can differ in epidemic persistence because of differences in the density dependence of susceptible recruitment. When recruitment is more strongly reduced at low susceptible densities, infection is less likely to survive until susceptibles recover. This makes the form of susceptible recruitment important when assessing whether an epidemic can persist in an animal population.

Our model and approximation have several limitations. The recruitment function \(\hth(x)\) is a simplified description of host population growth and does not explicitly capture all the demographic mechanisms operating in animal populations.

The asymptotic analysis also assumes slow susceptible recruitment, with \(\rhoeps\ll1\) and fixed \(\Rzero>1\). The asymptotic approximation to the first-wave epidemic trajectory assumes that the epidemic develops much faster than susceptibles are replenished. As \(\Rzero\) approaches one, infection grows more slowly, so susceptible replenishment may no longer be slow relative to epidemic growth. The approximation error may therefore increase.
% In addition, the deterministic description requires susceptible numbers to remain sufficiently large. Random fluctuations in susceptible numbers can increase the risk of extinction by depleting susceptibles to zero. In the stochastic model used here, recruitment vanishes at \(S=0\) for \(\theta>0\), preventing susceptible replenishment and leading to extinction of the remaining infection. Our approximation treats susceptible numbers deterministically and does not capture this route to extinction, so it can overestimate persistence when \(\Rzero\) is large.

Our analysis only considers whether a single-strain epidemic would go extinct in a well-mixed population and does not account for other epidemic dynamics such as spatial heterogeneity, metapopulation dynamics, or competition between pathogen strains.

These limitations suggest several directions for future work. A stochastic treatment of susceptible recovery and the entry state would allow the framework to address regimes in which fluctuations in susceptible numbers affect persistence. The framework could also be extended to allow the transmission coefficient \(\beta(N)\) to depend on total host population size \(N\).

Our results could also guide the development of models and large-scale simulations of more complex epidemic dynamics, such as metapopulation persistence, strain competition, and pathogen evolution.

\section{Second-order approximation}
\label{app:second-order}
We extend the first-order calculation by retaining the terms of order \(\rhoeps^2\) in the epidemic trajectory and the local corner approximation.

\subsection{Second-order correction to the epidemic trajectory}
We write
\begin{equation}
y(x;\rhoeps)=F(x)+\rhoeps Y_1(x)+\rhoeps^2Y_2(x)+O(\rhoeps^3).
\label{eq:outerexp-second-order}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:outerexp-second-order} and its derivative into \cref{eq:phaseplane} after multiplication by the denominator gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\Bigl[-\Rzero xF+\rhoeps\bigl(\hth-\Rzero xY_1\bigr)
-\rhoeps^2\Rzero xY_2\Bigr]
\Bigl[F'+\rhoeps Y_1'+\rhoeps^2Y_2'\Bigr]\\
&\qquad=(\Rzero x-1)\bigl[F+\rhoeps Y_1+\rhoeps^2Y_2\bigr]+O(\rhoeps^3),
\end{aligned}
\]
with all functions evaluated at \(x\).
At order \(\rhoeps^2\),
\[
-\Rzero xY_2F'+\bigl(\hth-\Rzero xY_1\bigr)Y_1'
-\Rzero xF Y_2'=(\Rzero x-1)Y_2.
\]
The terms containing \(Y_2\) cancel in the same way. Hence
\[
Y_2'(x)=\frac{\hth(x)-\Rzero xY_1(x)}{\Rzero xF(x)}Y_1'(x)
=\frac{\hth(x)(\Rzero x-1)}{\Rzero^2x^2F(x)^2}
\left[Y_1(x)-\frac{\hth(x)}{\Rzero x}\right].
\]
For invasion into a fully susceptible population with vanishing initial infectious fraction, the trajectory approaches \((1,0)\). Since \(F(1)=0\) and \(Y_1(1)=0\), the second-order correction satisfies \(Y_2(1)=0\). Thus,
\begin{align}
Y_2'(x)&=\frac{\hth(x)(\Rzero x-1)}{\Rzero^2x^2F(x)^2}
\left[Y_1(x)-\frac{\hth(x)}{\Rzero x}\right],
&Y_2(1)&=0.
\label{eq:Y2prime}
\end{align}
The first-order endpoint calculation gives \(Y_1(1-s)=s/\Rzero+O(s^2)\), where \(s=1-x\).
Since \cref{eq:initial-endpoint-expansions} also gives \(\hth(1-s)/[\Rzero(1-s)]=s/\Rzero+O(s^2)\), the leading terms cancel:
\begin{equation}
Y_1(1-s)-\frac{\hth(1-s)}{\Rzero(1-s)}=O(s^2).
\label{eq:Y2-initial-cancellation}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:initial-endpoint-expansions,eq:Y2-initial-cancellation} into \cref{eq:Y2prime}, the factor outside the brackets is \(O(s^{-1})\), while the bracketed difference is \(O(s^2)\). Hence \(Y_2'(1-s)=O(s)\). Both singularities are therefore removable, and extending the right-hand sides by continuity gives
\begin{equation}
Y_1'(1)=-\frac1{\Rzero},\qquad Y_2'(1)=0.
\label{eq:correction-endpoint-values}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Second-order matching and \texorpdfstring{\(\Dth\)}{D theta}}
We extend the inverse and corner expansions to
\begin{align}
X(y;\rhoeps)&=X_0(y)+\rhoeps X_1(y)+\rhoeps^2X_2(y)+O(\rhoeps^3),
\label{eq:inverseexp-second-order}\\
y&=\rhoeps v,\qquad x=\xf+\rhoeps u_1(v)+\rhoeps^2u_2(v)+\cdots.
\label{eq:corner-second-order}
\end{align}
Differentiating the corner expansion gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd x}{\dd y}=u_1'(v)+\rhoeps u_2'(v)+\cdots.
\label{eq:corner-derivative-second-order}
\end{equation}
We first determine the second-order outer coefficient \(X_2(y)\) and its behavior as \(y\to0^+\). We then derive the second-order corner correction \(u_2(v)\) and determine its integration constant by matching the two expansions.

Substituting the inverse expansion in \cref{eq:inverseexp-second-order} into \cref{eq:outerexp-second-order} and expanding about \(X_0(y)\) gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
y={}&F(X_0)\\
&+\rhoeps\left[F'(X_0)X_1+Y_1(X_0)\right]\\
&+\rhoeps^2\left[F'(X_0)X_2+\frac12F''(X_0)X_1^2
+Y_1'(X_0)X_1+Y_2(X_0)\right]+O(\rhoeps^3).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since \(F(X_0)=y\), the coefficients of each positive power of \(\rhoeps\) must vanish. The first-order coefficient gives \cref{eq:X1}, while the second-order coefficient gives
\[
F'(X_0)X_2+\frac12F''(X_0)X_1^2+Y_1'(X_0)X_1+Y_2(X_0)=0.
\]
Solving for \(X_2(y)\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
X_2(y)=
-\frac{\frac12F''(X_0)X_1^2+Y_1'(X_0)X_1+Y_2(X_0)}{F'(X_0)}.
\label{eq:X2general}
\end{equation}
To shorten the expressions below, we define
\begin{equation}
\delta_f=1-\Rzero\xf,\qquad h_f=\hth(\xf),\qquad h_f'=\hth'(\xf).
\label{eq:localdefs1}
\end{equation}
The coefficients already defined in \cref{eq:a,eq:b} then satisfy
\[
a=\frac{h_f}{\delta_f},\qquad b=\frac{\Rzero\xf}{\delta_f}.
\]
We also define
\begin{equation}
c=\delta_f h_f'+\Rzero h_f.
\label{eq:localdefs2}
\end{equation}
To calculate \(X_2(y)\) near \(y=0\), we need the corresponding expansions of \(Y_1(x)\) and \(Y_2(x)\) near \(\xf\). We first obtain the expansion of \(Y_1(x)\). Define
\begin{equation}
s=x-\xf,\qquad f_1=F'(\xf)=\frac{\delta_f}{\Rzero\xf},\qquad
f_2=F''(\xf)=-\frac{1}{\Rzero\xf^2}.
\label{eq:xf-local-derivatives}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
F(\xf+s)=f_1s+\frac12f_2s^2+O(s^3),\qquad
\hth(\xf+s)=h_f+h_f's+O(s^2).
\label{eq:xf-local-taylor}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:xf-local-taylor} into \cref{eq:Y1prime} and expanding the remaining factors gives
\begin{equation}
Y_1'(\xf+s)=\frac{h_f}{\Rzero\xf}\frac1s
+\frac{2\delta_f\xf h_f'-(1+2\delta_f)h_f}{2\Rzero\delta_f\xf^2}+O(s).
\label{eq:Y1prime-local}
\end{equation}
We denote these two coefficients by
\begin{equation}
\alpha=\frac{h_f}{\Rzero\xf},\qquad
p_0=\frac{2\delta_f\xf h_f'-(1+2\delta_f)h_f}{2\Rzero\delta_f\xf^2}.
\label{eq:p0}
\end{equation}
Integration therefore gives
\begin{equation}
Y_1(\xf+s)=\alpha\log s+\zeta+p_0s+O(s^2).
\label{eq:Y1localapp}
\end{equation}
The constant \(\zeta\) is determined by the first-order matching result. Using \cref{eq:Gtheta-finite-part,eq:Ctheta}, together with \(G_\theta=\Rzero Y_1\), gives
\begin{equation}
\zeta=\alpha\log\frac{f_1}{\Cth}.
\label{eq:zeta}
\end{equation}

We next identify the terms in \(Y_2'(x)\) whose integrals diverge as \(x\to\xf^+\). Let \(q_2(x)\) denote the right-hand side of \cref{eq:Y2prime}. Using \cref{eq:xf-local-taylor}, the second term in the square brackets of \cref{eq:Y2prime} expands as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\hth(\xf+s)}{\Rzero(\xf+s)}
&=\frac{h_f+h_f's+O(s^2)}{\Rzero\xf}
\left(1-\frac{s}{\xf}+O(s^2)\right)\\
&=\alpha+\left[\frac{h_f'}{\Rzero\xf}
-\frac{h_f}{\Rzero\xf^2}\right]s+O(s^2).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:recruitment-ratio-local}
\end{equation}
Subtracting \cref{eq:recruitment-ratio-local} from \cref{eq:Y1localapp} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Y_1(\xf+s)-\frac{\hth(\xf+s)}{\Rzero(\xf+s)}
={}&\alpha\log s+\zeta-\alpha\\
&+\left[p_0-\frac{h_f'}{\Rzero\xf}+\frac{h_f}{\Rzero\xf^2}\right]s+O(s^2).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:Y2-bracket-local}
\end{equation}
The prefactor in \cref{eq:Y2prime} expands as
\begin{equation}
\frac{\hth(x)(\Rzero x-1)}{\Rzero^2x^2F(x)^2}
=-\frac{a}{s^2}+\frac{h_f/\xf-c}{\delta_f^2}\frac1s+O(1).
\label{eq:Y2-prefactor-local}
\end{equation}
We define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
A_{-1}&=\frac{h_f/\xf-c}{\delta_f^2},\qquad r_0=\zeta-\alpha,\\
r_1&=p_0-\frac{h_f'}{\Rzero\xf}+\frac{h_f}{\Rzero\xf^2}
=-\frac{h_f}{2\Rzero\delta_f\xf^2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ABdefs}
\end{equation}
With these definitions, \cref{eq:Y2-prefactor-local,eq:Y2-bracket-local} become
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\hth(x)(\Rzero x-1)}{\Rzero^2x^2F(x)^2}
&=-\frac{a}{s^2}+\frac{A_{-1}}s+O(1),\\
Y_1(\xf+s)-\frac{\hth(\xf+s)}{\Rzero(\xf+s)}
&=\alpha\log s+r_0+r_1s+O(s^2).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:Y2-factors-abbreviated}
\end{equation}
Multiplying the two expansions in \cref{eq:Y2-factors-abbreviated} gives
\begin{equation}
q_2(\xf+s)=-\frac{a\alpha\log s+ar_0}{s^2}
+\frac{A_{-1}\alpha\log s+A_{-1}r_0-ar_1}{s}+O(|\log s|).
\label{eq:q2-local}
\end{equation}
We denote the displayed \(s^{-2}\) and \(s^{-1}\) terms by
\begin{equation}
q_{2,\rm sing}(s)=-\frac{a\alpha\log s+ar_0}{s^2}
+\frac{A_{-1}\alpha\log s+A_{-1}r_0-ar_1}{s}.
\label{eq:q2sing}
\end{equation}

We subtract the singular terms identified in \cref{eq:q2sing} and define
\begin{equation}
q_{2,\rm reg}(x)=q_2(x)-q_{2,\rm sing}(x-\xf).
\label{eq:q2reg}
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}
Y_2'(x)=q_{2,\rm sing}(x-\xf)+q_{2,\rm reg}(x).
\label{eq:Y2prime-split}
\end{equation}
By \cref{eq:q2-local}, \(q_{2,\rm reg}(\xf+s)=O(|\log s|)\). Since \(\int_0^\varepsilon|\log s|\dd s\) is finite, this remainder is integrable at \(\xf\). Integrating the singular terms gives the primitive
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
P_2(s)={}&\frac{a\alpha\log s+a\zeta}{s}
+\frac{A_{-1}\alpha}{2}\log^2s\\
&+(A_{-1}r_0-ar_1)\log s,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:P2}
\end{equation}
which satisfies \(P_2'(s)=q_{2,\rm sing}(s)\).

Integrating \cref{eq:Y2prime-split} with \(Y_2(1)=0\) from \cref{eq:Y2prime}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Y_2(x)&=-\int_x^1q_2(u)\dd u\\
&=-\int_x^1\left[q_{2,\rm sing}(u-\xf)+q_{2,\rm reg}(u)\right]\dd u\\
&=P_2(x-\xf)-P_2(1-\xf)-\int_x^1q_{2,\rm reg}(u)\dd u.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:Y2-regularized-integral}
\end{equation}
We therefore define
\begin{equation}
K_2=-P_2(1-\xf)-\int_{\xf}^{1}q_{2,\rm reg}(u)\dd u.
\label{eq:K2}
\end{equation}
Then \cref{eq:Y2-regularized-integral} becomes
\[
Y_2(x)=P_2(x-\xf)+K_2+\int_{\xf}^{x}q_{2,\rm reg}(u)\dd u.
\]
The remaining integral vanishes as \(x\to\xf^+\), giving
\begin{equation}
Y_2(x)=P_2(x-\xf)+K_2+o(1).
\label{eq:Y2-local}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Local expansions of the inverse trajectory.}
We now obtain the small-\(y\) expansions of \(X_0(y)\), \(X_1(y)\), and \(X_2(y)\) needed for second-order matching. With \(x=X_0(y)=\xf+s\), we first expand \(X_0(y)\).
Inverting \(y=f_1s+\tfrac12f_2s^2+O(s^3)\) from \cref{eq:xf-local-taylor} gives
\begin{equation}
X_0(y)-\xf=\frac{y}{f_1}-\frac{f_2}{2f_1^3}y^2+O(y^3)
=by+\frac{b\Rzero}{2\delta_f^2}y^2+O(y^3).
\label{eq:X0-second-order-local}
\end{equation}
Define
\[
W(s)=\alpha\log s+\zeta.
\]
Using \cref{eq:X1,eq:Y1localapp,eq:xf-local-taylor} gives
\begin{equation}
X_1(y)=-\frac{W(s)}{f_1}
+s\left[\frac{f_2W(s)}{f_1^2}-\frac{p_0}{f_1}\right]
+O(s^2|\log s|).
\label{eq:X1-local-second-order}
\end{equation}
Substituting \(s=by-f_2y^2/(2f_1^3)+O(y^3)\) from \cref{eq:X0-second-order-local} into \cref{eq:X1-local-second-order}, and using \cref{eq:zeta,eq:corner-L}, gives
\begin{equation}
X_1(y)=aL+y\left[\frac{\Rzero a}{\delta_f^2}(L+1)-\frac{bc}{\delta_f^2}\right]
+O(y^2|\log y|).
\label{eq:X1-second-order-local}
\end{equation}

We next determine \(X_2(y)\). Denote the numerator of \cref{eq:X2general} by
\begin{equation}
N_2(s)=\frac12F''(\xf+s)X_1(y)^2
+Y_1'(\xf+s)X_1(y)+Y_2(\xf+s).
\label{eq:N2-definition}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:X1-local-second-order,eq:Y1prime-local,eq:Y2-local,eq:P2} into \cref{eq:N2-definition} and using \(a=\alpha/f_1\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
N_2(s)={}&\frac{f_2W(s)^2}{2f_1^2}
-\frac{\alpha W(s)}{f_1s}
+\frac{\alpha f_2W(s)}{f_1^2}
-\frac{p_0[\alpha+W(s)]}{f_1}\\
&+\frac{aW(s)}s+\frac{A_{-1}\alpha}{2}\log^2s
+(A_{-1}r_0-ar_1)\log s+K_2+o(1)\\
={}&\frac{f_2W(s)^2}{2f_1^2}
+\frac{\alpha f_2W(s)}{f_1^2}
-\frac{p_0[\alpha+W(s)]}{f_1}\\
&+\frac{A_{-1}\alpha}{2}\log^2s
+(A_{-1}r_0-ar_1)\log s+K_2+o(1).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:N2-term-expansions}
\end{equation}
Collecting the logarithmic and constant terms in \cref{eq:N2-term-expansions} gives
\begin{equation}
N_2(s)=-\frac{f_1h_fc}{2\delta_f^3}\left[\log^2s+2\frac{\zeta}{\alpha}\log s\right]+N_{20}+o(1),
\label{eq:N2-local}
\end{equation}
where we define
\begin{align}
N_{20}&=K_2+\frac{\alpha\zeta f_2}{f_1^2}
-\frac{(\alpha+\zeta)p_0}{f_1}
+\frac{f_2\zeta^2}{2f_1^2},
\label{eq:N20}\\
\kappa&=\frac{ac}{2\delta_f^2}=\frac{h_fc}{2\delta_f^3}.
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{align}
Using \(F'(\xf+s)=f_1+O(s)\) and \cref{eq:N2-local} in \cref{eq:X2general}, we obtain
\[
X_2(y)=\kappa\left(\log s+\frac{\zeta}{\alpha}\right)^2
-\frac{N_{20}}{f_1}-\kappa\frac{\zeta^2}{\alpha^2}+o(1).
\]
We define the constant term as
\begin{equation}
\Dth=-\frac{N_{20}}{f_1}-\kappa\frac{\zeta^2}{\alpha^2}.
\label{eq:DthetaApp}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
X_2(y)=\kappa\left(\log s+\frac{\zeta}{\alpha}\right)^2+\Dth+o(1).
\label{eq:X2-local-Dtheta}
\end{equation}
By \cref{eq:xf-local-taylor},
\begin{equation}
y=f_1s+O(s^2)=f_1s\left[1+O(s)\right].
\label{eq:y-s-local}
\end{equation}
Since \(f_1>0\), \cref{eq:y-s-local} gives \(s=O(y)\). Using \cref{eq:zeta,eq:y-s-local}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\log s+\frac{\zeta}{\alpha}
&=\log\frac{f_1s}{\Cth}\\
&=\log\frac{y}{\Cth}-\log\left[1+O(s)\right]\\
&=\log\frac{y}{\Cth}+O(s)
=\log\frac{y}{\Cth}+O(y).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:log-s-to-y}
\end{equation}
Squaring \cref{eq:log-s-to-y} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\left(\log s+\frac{\zeta}{\alpha}\right)^2
&=\left[\log\frac{y}{\Cth}+O(y)\right]^2\\
&=\log^2\frac{y}{\Cth}
+O\left(y\left|\log\frac{y}{\Cth}\right|\right)+O(y^2)\\
&=\left(\log\frac{\Cth}{y}\right)^2+o(1),
\qquad y\to0^+,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:log-square-s-to-y}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:log-square-s-to-y} into \cref{eq:X2-local-Dtheta} therefore gives
\begin{equation}
X_2(y)=\kappa\left(\log\frac{\Cth}{y}\right)^2+\Dth+o(1),
\qquad y\to0^+.
\label{eq:X2asymp}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The second-order corner correction.}
We now determine \(u_2(v)\) by retaining the next order in the inverse phase-plane equation, \cref{eq:inverse-phaseplane}. The recruitment term is expanded about \(\xf\) as
\begin{equation}
\hth(x)=\hth(\xf)+\hth'(\xf)(x-\xf)+O\bigl((x-\xf)^2\bigr).
\label{eq:u2-recruitment-taylor}
\end{equation}
Substituting the corner expansion in \cref{eq:corner-second-order}, with \(y=\rhoeps v\), and using \cref{eq:corner-derivative-second-order,eq:u2-recruitment-taylor}, the terms needed to determine \(u_2'\) give
\begin{equation}
u_1'(v)+\rhoeps u_2'(v)
\approx
\frac{\rhoeps\left[\hth(\xf)-\Rzero\xf v\right]
+\rhoeps^2u_1(v)\left[\hth'(\xf)-\Rzero v\right]}
{\rhoeps(\Rzero\xf-1)v+\rhoeps^2\Rzero u_1(v)v}.
\label{eq:u2-substituted-quotient}
\end{equation}
Cancelling the common factor \(\rhoeps\) and multiplying by the denominator in \cref{eq:u2-substituted-quotient} yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\left[u_1'(v)+\rhoeps u_2'(v)\right]
\left[(\Rzero\xf-1)v+\rhoeps\Rzero u_1(v)v\right]\\
&\qquad\approx\hth(\xf)-\Rzero\xf v
+\rhoeps u_1(v)\left[\hth'(\xf)-\Rzero v\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:u2-product}
\end{equation}
Comparing the coefficients of \(\rhoeps\) in \cref{eq:u2-product} gives
\begin{equation}
(\Rzero\xf-1)v\,u_2'(v)+\Rzero u_1(v)v\,u_1'(v)
=u_1(v)\left[\hth'(\xf)-\Rzero v\right].
\label{eq:u2-coefficient}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:u1prime-full} into \cref{eq:u2-coefficient} and solving for \(u_2'(v)\) gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
u_2'(v)={}&-\frac{u_1(v)\left[\hth'(\xf)-\Rzero v\right]}{(1-\Rzero\xf)v}\\
&-\frac{\Rzero u_1(v)\left[\hth(\xf)-\Rzero\xf v\right]}{(1-\Rzero\xf)^2v}\\
={}&\frac{u_1(v)}{(1-\Rzero\xf)^2}
\left[\Rzero-\frac{(1-\Rzero\xf)\hth'(\xf)+\Rzero\hth(\xf)}{v}\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:u2prime-full}
\end{equation}
Then \cref{eq:u2prime-full} becomes
\begin{equation}
u_2'(v)=\frac{u_1(v)}{\delta_f^2}\left(\Rzero-\frac{c}{v}\right).
\label{eq:u2prime}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:u1} into \cref{eq:u2prime} gives
\[
u_2'(v)=-\frac{ac}{\delta_f^2}\frac{L}{v}
+\frac{\Rzero a}{\delta_f^2}L-\frac{bc}{\delta_f^2}
+\frac{b\Rzero}{\delta_f^2}v.
\]
Integrating with respect to \(v\) gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
u_2(v)={}&\frac{ac}{2\delta_f^2}L^2+B_2
+\frac{\Rzero a}{\delta_f^2}(L+1)v\\
&-\frac{bc}{\delta_f^2}v+\frac{b\Rzero}{2\delta_f^2}v^2.
\end{split}
\label{eq:u2pre}
\end{equation}
Here \(B_2\) is an undetermined constant arising from integration.

\paragraph{Matching the outer and corner expansions.}
We now determine \(B_2\) by matching the outer and corner expansions using \(y=\rhoeps v\). Using \cref{eq:X0-second-order-local,eq:X1-second-order-local,eq:X2asymp} in \cref{eq:inverseexp-second-order}, the terms needed for matching are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
x-\xf={}&by+\rhoeps aL+\frac{b\Rzero}{2\delta_f^2}y^2\\
&+\rhoeps y\left[\frac{\Rzero a}{\delta_f^2}(L+1)-\frac{bc}{\delta_f^2}\right]
+\rhoeps^2(\kappa L^2+\Dth)+\cdots.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:second-order-outer-matching}
\end{equation}
Matching is performed where \(y\to0^+\) and \(\rhoeps|\log y|\ll y\), so the outer correction remains smaller than the leading displacement. Substituting \(y=\rhoeps v\) into \cref{eq:second-order-outer-matching} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
x-\xf={}&\rhoeps(aL+bv)\\
&+\rhoeps^2\left[\kappa L^2+\Dth
+\frac{\Rzero a}{\delta_f^2}(L+1)v
-\frac{bc}{\delta_f^2}v
+\frac{b\Rzero}{2\delta_f^2}v^2\right]+\cdots.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:second-order-outer-local}
\end{equation}
The corner expansion in \cref{eq:corner-second-order}, with \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) from \cref{eq:u1,eq:u2pre}, has the same displayed terms except that \(\Dth\) is replaced by \(B_2\). Matching the remaining constant terms therefore determines
\begin{equation}
B_2=\Dth.
\label{eq:B2-matched}
\end{equation}
The matched second-order corner correction is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
u_2(v)={}&\kappa L^2+\Dth
+\frac{\Rzero a}{\delta_f^2}(L+1)v\\
&-\frac{bc}{\delta_f^2}v
+\frac{b\Rzero}{2\delta_f^2}v^2.
\end{split}
\label{eq:u2final}
\end{equation}
The constants \(\Cth\) and \(\Dth\) are determined by the first- and second-order outer solutions, respectively. Matching incorporates these constants into the local corner approximation near \(\xf\).

\subsection{Second-order entry estimate}
Evaluating the corner approximation at \(y=\yin\), with \(L=L_{\rm BL}\), gives the second-order entry estimate
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
x_{\rm in}^{(2)}=&\;\xf+\rhoeps aL_{\rm BL}+b\yin
+\rhoeps^2\left(\kappa L_{\rm BL}^2+\Dth\right)\\
&+\frac{\rhoeps\yin}{\delta_f^2}
\left[\Rzero a(L_{\rm BL}+1)-bc\right]
+\frac{b\Rzero}{2\delta_f^2}\yin^2.
\end{split}
\label{eq:xindir2}
\end{equation}
